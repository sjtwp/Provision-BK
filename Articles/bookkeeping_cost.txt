Author: Matthew Jacob
Date: November 13, 2026
ReadTime: 7 min

Summary:
"How much do you pay your bookkeeper?" is one of the most common questions small business owners ask each other, and the answers are all over the map. Some owners pay $150 a month, some pay $1,500, and many aren't sure whether they're overpaying. Here's what actually drives bookkeeping prices, typical monthly ranges, the pricing models you'll run into, and how to tell whether you're getting your money's worth.

Section:Why Bookkeeping Prices Vary So Much
Two businesses with the same revenue can pay very different amounts for bookkeeping. Price is driven by the work, not the size of your sales. The biggest factors are:
1.  Transaction volume: how many bank and credit card transactions need to be categorized each month.
2.  Number of accounts: every bank account, credit card, loan and merchant account has to be reconciled.
3.  Complexity: inventory, job costing, multiple locations, rental properties or several entities add time.
4.  Payroll and sales tax: running payroll and tying out Arizona transaction privilege tax (TPT) adds work and responsibility.
5.  How current your books are: monthly pricing assumes books are caught up. Months or years of backlog are quoted separately.
6.  What's included: data entry only, or reconciliations, financial statements and a monthly review of what the numbers mean.

Section:Typical Monthly Bookkeeping Costs
Every firm prices differently, but most small businesses fall into rough tiers like these:
1.  Very small or side business with one or two accounts and light activity: often $150 to $300 per month, and sometimes DIY software is enough.
2.  Established small business with a few accounts and steady activity: commonly $300 to $600 per month.
3.  Growing business with payroll, sales tax, job costing or several entities: often $600 to $1,500 or more per month.
Catch-up or cleanup work is usually a one-time project on top of the monthly fee, priced by how far behind the books are and how messy they are.

Section:Common Pricing Models
Hourly: you pay for time spent. It can look cheap at first, but bills change every month and owners often avoid asking questions because the meter is running.
Per-transaction or tiered: a base fee for up to a certain number of transactions, with an added charge for each block above it. Predictable as long as your volume is steady.
Flat monthly: a set price agreed up front, based on your accounts and volume. It's the easiest to budget and encourages you to ask questions. Most modern bookkeeping firms use this model.

Section:Signs You Might Be Overpaying
1.  Your bill changes every month and you can't tell why.
2.  You're paying for "bookkeeping" but your books are still months behind.
3.  You still do your own data entry or hunt down receipts.
4.  You get reports but nobody explains them.
5.  Your CPA charges extra at tax time to fix the books.
If you're paying for a service and still doing the work, or paying twice to clean it up, the real cost is higher than the invoice.

Section:Signs You Might Be Underpaying
Cheap bookkeeping can get expensive. Very low prices often mean data entry without reconciliations, no review of your numbers, or a bookkeeper juggling too many clients to answer your emails. The cost shows up later as IRS or Arizona Department of Revenue penalties, a big cleanup bill, or decisions made on numbers that were never right.

Section:When You Don't Need a Bookkeeper Yet
If your business is brand new or very small, with one bank account and a handful of transactions a month, you may not need a bookkeeper yet. A separate business bank account, QuickBooks Online with bank feeds turned on, and a monthly habit of reviewing your transactions can carry you for a while. Bring in help when the work starts taking hours each month, when you add payroll or sales tax, or when you need financial statements for a lender.

Section:What You Should Get for the Money
Whatever you pay, good bookkeeping should include every account reconciled each month, financial statements delivered on a predictable schedule, quick answers to your questions, and someone who explains what changed and why. The best value comes when your bookkeeper also gives you insight, like a light fractional CFO, pointing out where margins are slipping, which costs are creeping up and where you can make or save money.

Final: Bookkeeping isn't about finding the cheapest price. It's about paying a fair, predictable price for books you can trust and numbers you understand.
