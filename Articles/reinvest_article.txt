Title: Why Smart Business Owners Reinvest in Their Business and Hire Out the Rest
Author: Matthew Jacob
Date: November 9, 2026
ReadTime: 6 min
Image: reinvest_in_your_business.jpg


Summary:
Most successful small business owners put their extra cash back into their business before the stock market. The smartest ones also reinvest in their own time, handing off work like bookkeeping so they can focus on what they do best.

Section:What Owners Actually Do With Extra Cash
Ask a financial planner where your spare cash should go and you'll hear "diversify." Ask a successful small business owner and you'll usually hear something different: put it back into the business. The surveys back this up.
• In a 2017 survey of 400 U.S. small business owners by Kabbage and Bredin, <strong>40% said they would immediately reinvest extra cash in the business</strong>. Only 17% would put it toward retirement and 14% toward personal investments. (<a href="https://entrepreneur.com/starting-a-business/q-what-do-virtually-all-small-business-owners-have-in/306855" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Entrepreneur</a>)
• Research using Federal Reserve data finds entrepreneurs hold about <strong>40% of their financial wealth in business assets</strong>, often a single company they run themselves. (<a href="https://www.nber.org/papers/w8876" target="_blank" rel="noopener">NBER</a>)
• A Manta survey of 1,960 owners found <strong>34% have no retirement savings plan</strong>. Of those, 21% used their old retirement funds to start the business and 18% plan to fund retirement by selling it. (<a href="https://www.inc.com/john-swanciger/34-percent-of-small-business-owners-dont-have-a-re.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Inc.</a>)
• A 2020 Allica Bank study of 1,000 small businesses found the most successful firms reinvested more of their profits than weaker peers, and more reinvestment tracked with faster revenue growth. (<a href="https://startupsmagazine.co.uk/article-new-study-finds-smes-should-re-invest-9-profits-succeed" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Startups Magazine</a>)
For most owners, the business is the portfolio.

Section:Why They Do It: Depth Beats Spread
Owners reinvest because they want to get really, really good at one thing. An index fund spreads your money across hundreds of companies you'll never influence. Your business is the one investment where your effort, skill, and decisions move the result.
That's also why focus matters so much. Economists Tobias Moskowitz and Annette Vissing-Jørgensen found that, on average, money invested in private businesses has returned no more than public stocks, despite far more risk. The average business doesn't earn a premium. The owner who gets exceptionally good at their one thing is the one who does.
So the edge doesn't come from owning a business. It comes from mastering it. Every hour and dollar spent sharpening your core craft compounds. Every hour spread across tasks you'll never master dilutes it.
One honest caveat: reinvesting doesn't mean having no safety net. Many owners have nothing saved outside the business, and a sale is never guaranteed. Bet on your business, but keep a personal cushion beside it.

Section:Spend the Extra Cash to Buy Back Your Time
If you earn far more doing your core work than anything else, the best use of extra cash is often paying someone else to handle everything else. That isn't a luxury. It's the same reinvestment logic applied to your calendar.
The numbers show how much time is at stake. In a 2025 Upwork survey of 2,272 U.S. small business leaders, respondents said about <strong>30% of their working time</strong> goes to tasks outside their core skills. That works out to roughly 77 workdays a year. Most (58%) said those tasks crowd out strategic work, and 73% reported burnout. (<a href="https://ktvz.com/?p=1602152" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Upwork via KTVZ</a>)
Think of it as a simple trade. If your hour is worth $150 doing the work clients pay you for, every hour spent on a $50-an-hour task costs you $100. Distraction has a price, even when no check gets written.

Section:As the Business Grows, Hand Off What Isn't Your Craft
Doing everything yourself works when you're small. Once the business starts growing, you become the bottleneck. Tasks pile up faster than you can learn them, and each one done slowly steals hours from the work that actually grows revenue.
Owners already know this. In the same Upwork survey, 54% said delegating more would get better results, and 71% already knew which tasks they'd hand off first. The most common non-core tasks were customer support, admin, HR, social media, and bookkeeping.
A simple way to decide what goes first:
• <strong>It's repeatable.</strong> It happens every week or month, so a specialist can build a routine for it.
• <strong>Someone else does it faster.</strong> A pro who does it daily finishes in a fraction of your time, with fewer mistakes.
• <strong>Mistakes are expensive.</strong> Errors cost money, penalties, or bad decisions later.
• <strong>You dread it.</strong> If you keep putting it off, it's already costing you.
Bookkeeping checks every box.

Section:The DIY Bookkeeping Trap
I talk to a lot of business owners who nickel-and-dime their way into doing their own bookkeeping and accounting. It feels free because no check goes out. It isn't free. It's paid for in hours, stress, and mistakes.
• <strong>It's the part owners hate most.</strong> In SCORE research, 40% of owners named accounting and taxes the worst part of running a business. (<a href="https://www.accountingtoday.com/opinion/the-burden-of-accounting-and-taxes-on-small-business" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Accounting Today</a>)
• <strong>It eats whole weeks.</strong> The same SCORE data found 40% of owners spend more than 80 hours a year on tax prep alone. That's two full work weeks.
• <strong>It's bigger than tax season.</strong> Owners surveyed by Cornerstone Advisors in 2023 reported about 4 hours a week on bookkeeping alone, and over 20 hours a week across all finance tasks.
Now run the math. Say your time is worth $100 an hour and the books take you 10 hours a month. That's $1,000 a month of your time, or $12,000 a year. A professional bookkeeper who lives in QuickBooks every day does the same work in a fraction of the hours, usually for far less than that.
The hidden costs add up too. DIY books are often behind, so you make decisions on stale numbers. Miscategorized transactions turn into missed deductions or a messy cleanup at tax time. And paying a CPA by the hour to untangle a year of records costs far more than keeping them clean all year.

Final:
Successful owners bet on their business because it's the one investment they can make better. The same logic says to protect the hours that make it better. Reinvest in your craft, and pay professionals to handle the rest.
If you're still doing your own books, add up the hours you spent on them last month. Then compare that to what it would cost to hand them off. For most owners, it's far more affordable than they expect, and the time they get back is worth much more.
